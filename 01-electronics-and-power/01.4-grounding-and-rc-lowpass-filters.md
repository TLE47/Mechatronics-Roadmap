## Goal
Eliminate false analog sensor jumps when motors run by wiring a single-point **star ground** (preventing **ground bounce** $V_{\text{ground}} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{wire}}$), and size a passive **RC low-pass filter** with **cutoff frequency** $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$ to pass slow sensor changes while blocking high-frequency PWM switching noise.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **High-current ground bounce ($V_{\text{ground}} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{wire}}$) and star-ground wiring**: Why routing motor return current and analog sensor ground through the same breadboard jumper wire shifts the sensor's $0\text{ V}$ reference, and how meeting at one central battery ground terminal fixes it.
  2. **Passive RC low-pass filter cutoff frequency ($f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$) and high-frequency noise attenuation**: Using a resistor $R$ and capacitor $C$ so frequencies well below $f_c$ pass through ($\approx 100\%$), $f = f_c$ is reduced to $70.7\%$, and frequencies well above $f_c$ are squashed toward zero.
- **New syntax:** None (`math.pi` and `math.sqrt` from Lesson P.6).
- **Target time (unmeasured — [log yours](../.github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/beginner-timing-log.md)):** Typical: 1.5–2.0 hours | Slow-learner: 2.5–3.5 hours

### Idea 1: High-current ground bounce ($V_{\text{ground}} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{wire}}$) and star-ground wiring
- **Primary resource:** [Wikipedia — Ground loop ("Ground currents on signal cables" & "Solutions")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_loop_%28electricity%29) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Description`**, **`Ground currents on signal cables`**, and **`Solutions`** (single-point star grounding).
  - **What to skip:** Skip balanced audio transformer isolation.
  - **Key takeaway:** Real jumper wires are not $0.000\ \Omega$; a long thin breadboard jumper + spring clip can easily have $R_{\text{wire}} = 0.15\ \Omega$. If a $3.0\text{ A}$ motor return current and a sensitive analog sensor share that same ground wire back to the battery, Ohm's Law says the wire drops $V_{\text{ground}} = 3.0\text{ A} \times 0.15\ \Omega = 0.45\text{ V}$ (**ground bounce**)—making your Arduino's `analogRead(A0)` jump by $\sim 92$ counts every time the motor turns on! **Star grounding** solves this by running **separate** ground wires from (1) the high-current motor driver, (2) the Arduino logic board, and (3) the analog sensors so they meet at **one single star point** at the negative battery terminal.

### Idea 2: Passive RC low-pass filter cutoff frequency ($f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$) and high-frequency noise attenuation
- **Primary resource:** [Wikipedia — Low-pass filter ("RC filter" & "Time response")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pass_filter) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Low-pass filter`**, **`Examples`**, and **`Time response`** (connecting the RC time constant $\tau = R C$ to cutoff frequency $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$).
  - **What to skip:** Skip complex Laplace transfer functions and decibel ($\text{dB}$) logarithmic plots (covered in Pass 2).
  - **Key takeaway:** When you wire a series resistor $R$ between a noisy sensor and an Arduino ADC pin and place a capacitor $C$ from the ADC pin to `GND`, the capacitor acts like a fast shock absorber (`Lesson S0.6`). Slow sensor voltages pass straight through to the ADC pin, while fast PWM switching spikes are shorted into the capacitor before they can move the pin voltage! The boundary between "slow" and "fast" is the **cutoff frequency** $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$. At input frequency $f$, the output-to-input amplitude ratio is $\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{in}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f / f_c)^2}}$:
    - **Slow signal ($f \ll f_c$):** Ratio $\approx 1.00$ ($100\%$ passes through cleanly).
    - **At cutoff ($f = f_c$):** Ratio $= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \mathbf{0.707}$ ($70.7\%$ amplitude).
    - **Fast noise ($f = 10 \cdot f_c$):** Ratio $= \frac{1}{\sqrt{101}} \approx \mathbf{0.0995}$ (over $90\%$ of the noise amplitude is wiped out!).
  - **Alternative resource:** [Falstad Circuit Simulator — RC Low-Pass Filter](https://www.falstad.com/circuit/circuitjs.html) `[verify in-app]` — Import [`falstad/01.4-rc-lowpass.txt`](falstad/01.4-rc-lowpass.txt) `[verify in-app]` to see a $1\text{ k}\Omega$ + $1\ \mu\text{F}$ filter ($f_c = 159.15\text{ Hz}$) attenuate a cutoff sine wave to $70.7\%$.

### Bridge Notes
In a noisy robot, clean analog sensing requires two defenses working together:
1. **Star Grounding (eliminates shared ground-wire voltage drop):**
   If motor current $I_{\text{motor}}$ flows through a shared wire of resistance $R_{\text{shared}}$ between the sensor ground and the Arduino `GND`, the ADC sees a false offset error $V_{\text{ground}}-\text{bounce} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{shared}}$. Wiring a dedicated sensor ground directly to the Arduino `GND` pin (so $I_{\text{motor}}$ never flows through the sensor's ground wire) reduces $V_{\text{ground}}-\text{bounce}$ on the sensor reading to $0.00\text{ V}$.

2. **Passive RC Low-Pass Filter (blocks high-frequency switching noise):**
   With $R = 1{,}000\ \Omega$ ($1\text{ k}\Omega$) and $C = 1.0\ \mu\text{F}$ ($10^{-6}\text{ F}$), the time constant is $\tau = R C = 0.001\text{ s}$ ($1.0\text{ ms}$) and the **cutoff frequency** is $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 0.001\text{ s}} \approx 159.15\text{ Hz}$, with amplitude ratio $\text{Ratio}(f) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f / f_c)^2}}$. A slow $2\text{ Hz}$ knob turn passes at $99.99\%$ amplitude, while $20{,}000\text{ Hz}$ ($20\text{ kHz}$) motor PWM noise is crushed to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (20000/159.15)^2}} \approx 0.008$ ($0.8\%$—over $99\%$ blocked!).

import math


def ground_bounce_adc_error(i_motor_a, r_shared_ohm, v_ref=5.0):

    adc_count_error = (v_bounce / v_ref) * 1023.0

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Picking an RC cutoff frequency $f_c$ that is *too low* (like $f_c = 0.5\text{ Hz}$ on a fast robot joint sensor):** Remember from `Lesson 00.1` that $\tau = R C = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c}$. If $f_c = 0.5\text{ Hz}$, then $\tau \approx 0.32\text{ s}$ ($5\tau \approx 1.6\text{ s}$), making your sensor reading lag behind the real arm position by over a second! Choose $f_c$ roughly $5\times\text{–}10\times$ higher than your fastest physical motion, and well below the PWM switching frequency.
- **Daisy-chaining sensor `GND` to the H-bridge motor driver `GND` pin:** Always wire analog sensor `GND` directly to an Arduino `GND` pin, and run a separate heavy wire from the H-bridge `GND` straight to the battery negative terminal (**star ground**).

## Check yourself

1. **Star ground vs. daisy-chain ground:** Why does a potentiometer reading on `A0` jump by decenas of counts when a motor starts if the potentiometer's `GND` leg is plugged into the H-bridge's ground row on a breadboard?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   The motor's multi-amp return current flows through the resistance of that shared breadboard ground jumper ($V_{\text{ground}} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{wire}}$), lifting the potentiometer's $0\text{ V}$ reference pin above the Arduino's `GND` (**ground bounce**).
   </details>

2. **Calculating RC cutoff frequency $f_c$:** What is the cutoff frequency $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$ of a low-pass filter built from $R = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ ($10{,}000\ \Omega$) and $C = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ($10^{-7}\text{ F}$)?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   $\tau = R \cdot C = 10{,}000 \times 10^{-7} = 0.001\text{ s}$, so $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 0.001} \approx \mathbf{159.15\text{ Hz}}$—the exact same cutoff frequency as $1\text{ k}\Omega$ + $1\ \mu\text{F}$!
   </details>

## Recap
- **Ground bounce** ($V_{\text{ground}} = I_{\text{motor}} \cdot R_{\text{wire}}$) corrupts sensor readings when motor return current shares a ground wire with sensors; **star grounding** gives motor power, logic, and sensors separate ground paths meeting at the battery negative terminal.
- A passive **RC low-pass filter** has **cutoff frequency** $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}$ where signal amplitude is $70.7\%$, passing slow sensor changes ($f \ll f_c$) while attenuating fast switching noise ($f \gg f_c$).
