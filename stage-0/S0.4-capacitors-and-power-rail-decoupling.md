## Learn

### Idea 1: Capacitor Charge Storage ($C$) and the $RC$ Time Constant ($\tau = R \cdot C$)
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Capacitors](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/capacitors) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Introduction`**, **`Symbols and Units`**, **` Capacitor Theory`**, and **`Charge and Discharge`** (under **`Application Examples`**).
  - **What to skip:** Skip **`Capacitors in Series/Parallel`** and **`Supercapacitors`**.
  - **Key takeaway:** A **capacitor** consists of two conductive plates separated by an insulating gap (dielectric); its size (**capacitance** $C$) is measured in **Farads (`F`)**, typically in **microfarads (`µF`, $10^{-6}\text{ F}$)** or **nanofarads (`nF`, $10^{-9}\text{ F}$)**. Because it takes time for current $I$ to fill or empty the plates, **the voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously**—when charged through a resistor $R$, its voltage rises along a smooth curve governed by the **$RC$ time constant** $\tau = R \cdot C$ (in seconds), reaching $\approx 63.2\%$ of the step change in $1\,\tau$ and $>99\%$ in $5\,\tau$.
  - **Alternative resource:** [Falstad Circuit Simulator — RC Time Constant](https://www.falstad.com/circuit/circuitjs.html) `[HTTP 200 — SPA/App: verify in-app]` — Open `Circuits` $\rightarrow$ `Basics` $\rightarrow$ `Capacitor` `[verify in-app]` and click the switch to watch the green voltage trace curve smoothly up and down on the bottom oscilloscope while steady DC current stops once the capacitor is full.

### Idea 2: Power-Rail Decoupling (Bypass) Capacitors and Polarized Capacitor Safety
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Capacitors ("Decoupling" and "Types of Capacitors")](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/capacitors) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Types of Capacitors`**, **`Ceramic Capacitors`**, **`Aluminum Electrolytic`**, and **`Decoupling (Bypass) Capacitors`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip **`Power Factor Correction`**.
  - **Key takeaway:** When a motor or digital chip suddenly switches on, long power wires cannot deliver current instantly, causing the `5V` supply rail to sag (a **brownout** that resets the microcontroller). Placing a **decoupling capacitor** (typically a $100\text{ nF} = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ **ceramic capacitor** plus a $100\ \mu\text{F}$ **electrolytic capacitor**) in parallel across `5V` and `GND` acts like a local water tower that supplies instant current and holds the voltage steady. Ceramic capacitors are **unpolarized** (either leg can go to `GND`), whereas cylindrical aluminum **electrolytic capacitors are polarized**—their negative (`-`) stripe leg **must** connect to `GND`, or internal gas pressure will pop the metal can!
  - **Alternative resource:** [PhET — Capacitor Lab: Basics](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/capacitor-lab-basics) `[HTTP 200 — SPA/App: verify in-app]` — Open **`Light Bulb`** `[verify in-app]`, charge the capacitor with the battery, and flip the switch to watch the stored charge light the bulb.

### Bridge Notes
In the water-pipe analogy, a **capacitor** is a flexible rubber diaphragm (or local pressure tank) connected across the supply and return pipes. Under steady **DC** conditions, once the tank fills up to the supply voltage ($5.0\text{ V}$), no more current flows into it—to steady DC, a fully charged capacitor acts like an **open circuit**. However, whenever the supply voltage tries to spike up or sag down suddenly, the capacitor absorbs or releases charge ($Q = C \cdot V$) to resist the change and smooth the voltage out!

Because base unit **1 Farad (`1 F`)** is huge, real circuits use:
- **Microfarads (`µF`):** $1\ \mu\text{F} = 10^{-6}\text{ F}$ (e.g., $100\ \mu\text{F} = 0.0001\text{ F}$ bulk electrolytic tank capacitor).
- **Nanofarads (`nF`):** $1\text{ nF} = 10^{-9}\text{ F} = 0.001\ \mu\text{F}$ (e.g., $100\text{ nF} = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ceramic bypass capacitor).

When a capacitor $C$ charges or discharges through resistance $R$, the product $\tau = R \cdot C$ (in seconds) is called the **$RC$ time constant**:
- After **$1\,\tau$**, the capacitor reaches **$63.2\%$** of its final voltage change.
- After **$5\,\tau$**, it reaches **$99.3\%$** (considered fully charged or discharged in engineering).

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Wiring an electrolytic capacitor backward (`-` stripe connected to `+5V`):** Aluminum electrolytic capacitors contain a liquid/gel electrolyte that forms an insulating oxide layer only when positive voltage is on the anode (`+`, longer leg) and the negative stripe (`-`, shorter leg) is on `GND`. Reversing the polarity breaks down the oxide, boils the electrolyte into gas, and pops the metal cylinder! (See [Safety Guide](../resources/safety.md)).
- **Placing a decoupling capacitor far away on the other side of the breadboard:** A decoupling capacitor only prevents voltage droop if it is wired **right next to** the chip or motor driver's `VCC` and `GND` pins with short leads.
- **Wiring a decoupling capacitor in series instead of in parallel:** Because a capacitor blocks steady DC once charged, putting it *in series* with a `5V` power wire cuts off DC power to your circuit! Decoupling capacitors always go **in parallel** between `5V` and `GND`.

1. **Calculating the $RC$ time constant:** You pair a $10\text{ k}\Omega$ ($10{,}000\ \Omega$) resistor with a $10\ \mu\text{F}$ ($0.00001\text{ F}$) capacitor. What is the time constant $\tau = R \cdot C$ in seconds (`s`) and in milliseconds (`ms`), and roughly how long ($5\,\tau$) does it take to charge to $>99\%$?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   - $\tau = 10{,}000\ \Omega \times 0.00001\text{ F} = \mathbf{0.10\text{ s}} = \mathbf{100.0\text{ ms}}$.
   - $5\,\tau = 5 \times 100.0\text{ ms} = \mathbf{500.0\text{ ms}}$ ($0.5\text{ s}$).
   </details>

2. **Steady-state DC behavior:** Once a capacitor connected to a $5.0\text{ V}$ DC supply has finished charging to $5.0\text{ V}$, how much steady DC current continues to flow into the capacitor?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   **`0.0 A` (zero current)**. Because the capacitor plates are now at the exact same $5.0\text{ V}$ pressure as the supply ($V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{cap}} = 0\text{ V}$), no more charge flows—a charged capacitor acts as an **open circuit** to steady DC.
   </details>

3. **Polarized vs. unpolarized:** Which common capacitor type ($100\text{ nF}$ small yellow/brown **ceramic** disc vs. $100\ \mu\text{F}$ cylindrical aluminum **electrolytic** can) is **polarized** and will burst if wired backward?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   The cylindrical **aluminum electrolytic capacitor** is polarized (always connect its `-` stripe side to `GND`). Ceramic capacitors are unpolarized and can be inserted either way.
   </details>

## Recap
- A **capacitor** ($C$, in Farads `µF`/`nF`) stores charge on separated plates and resists sudden changes in voltage, charging or discharging with **$RC$ time constant** $\tau = R \cdot C$.
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitors** placed in parallel across `5V` and `GND` supply instant local current to prevent microcontroller brownouts.
