# Lesson S0.2: Series vs. Parallel Resistor Networks, Voltage Dividers and Potentiometers

## Goal
Distinguish **series** (end-to-end, shared current) from **parallel** (side-by-side, shared voltage) connections, and use the **voltage divider formula** ($V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$) in Falstad to scale voltages and read 3-pin **potentiometers**.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **Series versus parallel circuits**: Why components in **series** share the exact same current ($I_{\text{total}} = I_1 = I_2$, $R_{\text{series}} = R_1 + R_2$, voltages add up to $V_{\text{supply}}$), whereas components in **parallel** share the exact same voltage ($V_1 = V_2 = V_{\text{supply}}$, branch currents add up).
  2. **Resistive voltage dividers and 3-pin potentiometers**: Using two series resistors ($R_1$ on top, $R_2$ on bottom to `GND`) to produce an output voltage $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$, and how a rotating **potentiometer** wiper sweeps $V_{\text{out}}$ continuously from $0\text{ V}$ to $V_{\text{in}}$.
- **New syntax:** Falstad voltage probe (`p`) element in circuit netlists.
- **Estimated time (untimed guesses):** Typical: 1.5–2.0 hours | Slow-learner: 2.5–3.5 hours

## Learn

### Idea 1: Series versus parallel circuits
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Series and Parallel Circuits](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/series-and-parallel-circuits) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Series Circuits`**, **`Parallel Circuits`**, and **`Calculating Equivalent Resistance`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip the multi-loop series-parallel combination exercises at the very bottom.
  - **Key takeaway:** In a **series circuit**, components are chained end-to-end along a single path: the **current is identical** everywhere ($I_1 = I_2$), total resistance adds ($R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$), and the supply voltage splits across the resistors ($V_{\text{in}} = V_1 + V_2$). In a **parallel circuit**, components are wired side-by-side across the same two nodes: the **voltage across every branch is identical** ($V_1 = V_2 = V_{\text{in}}$), and the total supply current is the sum of the branch currents ($I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2$).

### Idea 2: Resistive voltage dividers and 3-pin potentiometers
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Voltage Dividers](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/voltage-dividers) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Ideal Voltage Divider`**, **`Voltage Divider Equation`**, and **`Applications`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip the resistor-color-code lookup table.
  - **Key takeaway:** Two series resistors ($R_1$ connected to $V_{\text{in}}$, $R_2$ connected to `GND`) form a **voltage divider** whose middle node sits at $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$. A 3-pin **potentiometer** is simply a variable voltage divider inside a knob: its two outer pins connect to `5V` and `GND`, and turning the knob slides the middle **wiper** pin smoothly from $0.0\text{ V}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$.
  - **Alternative resource:** [Falstad Circuit Simulator — Potentiometer Divider](https://www.falstad.com/circuit/circuitjs.html) `[verify in-app]` — Open `Circuits` $\rightarrow$ `Basics` $\rightarrow$ `Potentiometer` `[verify in-app]` and drag the **`Resistance`** slider on the right pane `[verify in-app]` to sweep the wiper voltage.

### Bridge Notes
Let's derive the **voltage divider formula** in two steps from Idea 1 and Ohm's Law (`Lesson S0.1`):
1. Because $R_1$ (top resistor, connected to $V_{\text{in}}$) and $R_2$ (bottom resistor, connected to `GND`) are in **series**, their total resistance is $R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$, so the series current is $I = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_1 + R_2}$.
2. The output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ (measured from the middle node between $R_1$ and $R_2$ down to `GND`) is the voltage drop across the **bottom resistor $R_2$**:

$$
V_{\text{out}} = I \cdot R_2 = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

Notice two essential sanity checks:
- **Equal resistors ($R_1 = R_2$):** $\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$, so $V_{\text{out}}$ is always **half** of $V_{\text{in}}$ ($2.50\text{ V}$ from a $5.0\text{ V}$ supply).
- **Safe battery-monitoring divider ($12\text{ V} \rightarrow 3.84\text{ V}$):** A $12.0\text{ V}$ motor battery will destroy a $5.0\text{ V}$ Arduino pin if wired directly! Using $R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ on top and $R_2 = 4.7\text{ k}\Omega$ on the bottom scales $12.0\text{ V}$ down to $12.0 \times \frac{4700}{10000 + 4700} \approx 3.84\text{ V}$, which is safely below the $5.0\text{ V}$ pin limit.

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Putting $R_1$ in the numerator instead of $R_2$ ($V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}$):** Because $V_{\text{out}}$ is measured from the middle node to **`GND`**, the numerator is always the resistor connected to **`GND`** ($R_2$). If you swap $R_1$ and $R_2$ on a $12\text{ V}$ battery divider ($4.7\text{ k}\Omega$ on top, $10\text{ k}\Omega$ on bottom), $V_{\text{out}}$ jumps to $8.16\text{ V}$—which exceeds a $5.0\text{ V}$ microcontroller pin limit!
- **Trying to power a heavy load (like a motor) from a voltage divider:** A voltage divider is only for **low-current sensor signals**! As soon as a heavy load draws current from the middle node, the output voltage collapses.

## Check yourself

1. **Series vs. parallel resistors:** You have two $1{,}000\ \Omega$ ($1\text{ k}\Omega$) resistors. What is their combined resistance if you wire them in **series**, and what is their combined resistance if you wire them in **parallel**?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   - **In series:** $R_{\text{series}} = 1{,}000 + 1{,}000 = \mathbf{2{,}000\ \Omega}$ ($2.0\text{ k}\Omega$).
   - **In parallel:** Two identical resistors in parallel offer twice the cross-sectional path, cutting resistance in half to $\frac{1{,}000 \times 1{,}000}{1{,}000 + 1{,}000} = \mathbf{500\ \Omega}$.
   </details>

2. **Voltage divider calculation:** A $5.0\text{ V}$ supply feeds a top resistor $R_1 = 3.0\text{ k}\Omega$ in series with a bottom resistor $R_2 = 1.0\text{ k}\Omega$ to `GND`. What is $V_{\text{out}}$ at the middle node?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   $V_{\text{out}} = 5.0\text{ V} \times \frac{1.0}{3.0 + 1.0} = 5.0 \times 0.25 = \mathbf{1.25\text{ V}}$.
   </details>

3. **Potentiometer extremes:** A $10\text{ k}\Omega$ potentiometer has its outer pins connected to `5V` and `GND`. What voltage appears on the middle wiper pin when turned all the way toward `GND`, halfway (`50%`), and all the way toward `5V`?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   **$0.00\text{ V}$** at the `GND` end, **$2.50\text{ V}$** at the midpoint (`50%`), and **$5.00\text{ V}$** at the `5V` end.
   </details>

## Recap
- **Series** components share the same current ($R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$); **parallel** components share the same voltage.
- A **voltage divider** outputs $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$, and a **potentiometer** is a 3-pin adjustable voltage divider.
