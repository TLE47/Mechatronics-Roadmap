# Lesson S0.4: Multimeters (Voltmeter vs. Ammeter) and Current-Limited (`CC/CV`) Bench Supplies

## Goal
Measure circuit voltage **in parallel** and current **in series** without blowing a multimeter fuse, and use a **Constant-Voltage / Constant-Current (`CC/CV`)** bench power supply limit so accidental wiring shorts fold back voltage safely instead of burning components.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **Parallel voltmeter versus series ammeter probing**: Why a voltmeter has huge internal resistance ($\approx 10\text{ M}\Omega$) and probes **across** two points, whereas an ammeter has near-zero internal resistance ($\approx 0.1\ \Omega$) and must be inserted **in series** by breaking the loop.
  2. **Current-limited (`CC/CV`) bench power supplies**: How setting both a target voltage (`CV`, e.g., $5.0\text{ V}$) and a maximum current ceiling (`CC`, e.g., $0.10\text{ A}$) automatically drops supply voltage to near zero if a short circuit occurs.
- **New syntax:** None (uses Python `if/else` and Ohm's Law from Track P and S0.1).
- **Estimated time (untimed guesses):** Typical: 1.0–1.5 hours | Slow-learner: 2.0–3.0 hours

## Learn

### Idea 1: Parallel voltmeter versus series ammeter probing
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — How to Use a Multimeter](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-use-a-multimeter) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Parts of a Multimeter`**, **`Measuring Voltage`**, **`Measuring Resistance`**, **`Measuring Current`**, **`Continuity`**, and **`Changing the Fuse`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip product comparison tables at the top and bottom.
  - **Key takeaway:** A multimeter combines three tools: (1) **Continuity / Resistance ($\Omega$)** tests whether two points are connected with power **OFF** (beeping if $R \approx 0\ \Omega$), (2) a **Voltmeter (`V`)** has huge internal resistance ($\approx 10\text{ M}\Omega$) and touches **in parallel** across a component, and (3) an **Ammeter (`A`/`mA`)** has near-zero resistance and must be wired **in series** by breaking the circuit loop. Touching an ammeter across a battery or `5V` rail creates a dead short through the meter and blows its fuse!

### Idea 2: Current-limited (`CC/CV`) bench power supplies
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — How to Power a Project ("Variable DC Benchtop Power Supply")](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/how-to-power-a-project) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]` & [Wikipedia — Current Limiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_limiting) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** On the SparkFun page, read exact headings **`Variable DC Benchtop Power Supply`** and **`Voltage/Current Considerations`**. On the Wikipedia page, read **`Current limiting`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip **`Powering LilyPad LED Projects`** and **`Inrush current limiting`**.
  - **Key takeaway:** Unlike a raw battery (which dumps 50+ Amps into a short circuit until wires melt), a **benchtop DC power supply** lets you set two dials before turning output on: a **Voltage setpoint ($V_{\text{set}}$)** and a **Current limit ($I_{\text{limit}}$)**. During normal operation ($I_{\text{load}} < I_{\text{limit}}$), the supply stays in **Constant Voltage (`CV`)** mode at $V_{\text{set}}$. If a wiring mistake causes load resistance $R_{\text{load}}$ to drop so low that $V_{\text{set}} / R_{\text{load}} > I_{\text{limit}}$, the supply automatically switches into **Constant Current (`CC`)** mode—clamping current at $I_{\text{limit}}$ and folding output voltage down to $V_{\text{out}} = I_{\text{limit}} \cdot R_{\text{load}}$ so nothing burns!

### Bridge Notes
Before powering up any circuit, professional engineers follow a two-step safety ritual using their **multimeter** and **current-limited (`CC/CV`) power supply**:

1. **Power-Off Continuity Check & Probing Rule:**
   - With power **OFF**, set your multimeter to **Continuity** and touch the probes between `5V` and `GND`. If it does **not** beep, there is no dead short.
   - Keep the red probe in the **`V / Ω` socket**. When measuring **voltage**, touch the probes **across** (in parallel with) the component. When measuring **current**, you must break the circuit wire so all current flows **through** the meter in series (or in Falstad, simply hover your mouse over any wire or insert `Outputs and Labels -> Add Ammeter` in series and `Add Voltmeter/Scope Probe` in parallel).

2. **CC/CV Power-Up Math:**
   - Suppose you set a bench supply to $V_{\text{set}} = 5.0\text{ V}$ and $I_{\text{limit}} = 0.10\text{ A}$ ($100\text{ mA}$).
   - Connected to a normal $1{,}000\ \Omega$ circuit, Ohm's Law asks for $I = \frac{5.0}{1000} = 0.005\text{ A} \le 0.10\text{ A}$, so the supply stays in **`CV` mode** at $5.00\text{ V}$.
   - Connected to an accidental $0.05\ \Omega$ short circuit, Ohm's Law would ask for $100\text{ A} > 0.10\text{ A}$, so the supply flips into **`CC` mode**, limits current to $I = 0.10\text{ A}$, and drops terminal voltage to $V_{\text{out}} = 0.10\text{ A} \times 0.05\ \Omega = 0.005\text{ V}$ (dissipating a harmless $0.5\text{ mW}$ instead of $500\text{ W}$!).

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Touching an Ammeter in parallel across `5V` and `GND`:** Because an ammeter's current jack has near-zero internal resistance ($\approx 0.1\ \Omega$), touching its probes across a voltage source shorts the supply straight through the meter and blows its internal fuse! Always measure voltage in parallel (red lead in `V/Ω` jack) and current in series.
- **Measuring resistance or continuity while the circuit is powered ON:** Multimeter resistance/continuity mode injects a tiny test voltage; if the circuit is already powered on, the meter reads false numbers or can be damaged. Always turn power **OFF** before checking continuity.

## Check yourself

1. **Voltmeter vs. Ammeter wiring:** Which meter mode (Voltmeter or Ammeter) is connected **in parallel** across a component without breaking the circuit, and which has near-zero internal resistance and must be wired **in series**?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   A **Voltmeter** (high resistance, $\approx 10\text{ M}\Omega$) connects **in parallel** across a component; an **Ammeter** (near-zero resistance) must be wired **in series** by breaking the loop.
   </details>

2. **CC/CV mode transition:** Your bench power supply is set to $V_{\text{set}} = 12.0\text{ V}$ and $I_{\text{limit}} = 0.50\text{ A}$. If a motor stalls and acts like a $4.0\ \Omega$ resistor, does the supply stay in `CV` mode or enter `CC` mode, and what voltage appears across the motor terminals?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   At $12.0\text{ V}$, a $4.0\ \Omega$ load would demand $I = 12.0 / 4.0 = 3.0\text{ A} > 0.50\text{ A}$. The supply immediately enters **`CC` (Constant-Current) mode**, caps current at **$0.50\text{ A}$**, and drops terminal voltage to $V_{\text{out}} = 0.50\text{ A} \times 4.0\ \Omega = \mathbf{2.0\text{ V}}$.
   </details>

3. **Pre-flight continuity check:** Before turning on power to a freshly wired breadboard, you set your multimeter to **Continuity** and touch the probes to the `5V` and `GND` rails. Do you want the meter to beep or stay silent?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   **Stay silent!** A beep between `5V` and `GND` means $R \approx 0\ \Omega$—a dead short circuit between power and ground that must be fixed before applying power.
   </details>

## Recap
- Probe **voltage in parallel** (`V/Ω` jack), probe **current in series** (breaking the loop), and check **continuity with power OFF**.
- A **current-limited (`CC/CV`) bench power supply** stays at $V_{\text{set}}$ during normal operation (`CV`) and folds voltage down to $I_{\text{limit}} \cdot R_{\text{load}}$ (`CC`) during a short circuit or overload.
