# Lesson S0.6: Capacitors — Charge Storage ($\tau = RC$) and Power-Rail Decoupling

## Goal
Calculate how fast a **capacitor** charges or discharges through a resistor using the **RC time constant** ($\tau = R \cdot C$), and place a $100\text{ nF}$ ($0.1\ \mu\text{F}$) **decoupling capacitor** across a microcontroller's power pins to prevent motor current spikes from resetting the chip.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **Capacitor charge storage and RC time constant ($\tau = R \cdot C$)**: How two conductive plates separated by an insulator store charge in Farads (`F`, `µF`, `nF`) and resist sudden changes in **voltage** with characteristic time $\tau = R \cdot C$ ($63.2\%$ in $1\tau$, $\approx 99.3\%$ in $5\tau$).
  2. **Power-rail decoupling (bypass) capacitors**: Combining a fast $0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ceramic capacitor and a larger $100\ \mu\text{F}$ bulk electrolytic capacitor in parallel across `VCC` and `GND` as a local charge reservoir.
- **New syntax:** `math.exp(-t / tau)` for exponential charging curves in Python.
- **Estimated time (untimed guesses):** Typical: 1.5–2.0 hours | Slow-learner: 2.5–3.0 hours

### Idea 1: Capacitor charge storage and RC time constant ($\tau = R \cdot C$)
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Capacitors](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/capacitors) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`

  - **What to skip:** Skip **`Capacitors in Series/Parallel`** and **`Application Examples -> Signal Filtering`**.
  - **Key takeaway:** A **capacitor** acts like a tiny, ultra-fast rechargeable bucket that stores electrical charge on two conductive plates separated by an insulator, measured in **Farads (`F`)**—typically **microfarads (`µF`, $10^{-6}\text{ F}$)** or **nanofarads (`nF`, $10^{-9}\text{ F}$)**. Because moving charge onto the plates takes time, **a capacitor's voltage cannot change instantly**. When charged through a resistor $R$, the **RC time constant** $\tau = R \cdot C$ (in seconds) tells you the exact time needed to cover $63.2\%$ of the remaining voltage step (reaching $\approx 99.3\%$ after $5\tau$).
  - **Alternative resource:** [PhET — Capacitor Lab: Basics](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/capacitor-lab-basics) `[verify in-app]` — Connect the battery to the parallel plates `[verify in-app]` to watch charge accumulate until $V_C = V_{\text{battery}}$.

### Idea 2: Power-rail decoupling (bypass) capacitors
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Capacitors ("Decoupling (Bypass) Capacitors" & "Power Supply Filtering")](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/capacitors) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Application Examples`**, **`Decoupling (Bypass) Capacitors`**, and **`Power Supply Filtering`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip audio crossover sections.
  - **Key takeaway:** When a motor or digital chip suddenly demands a burst of current, the resistance and inductance of long battery wires cause the $5.0\text{ V}$ supply rail to dip ("brown out"), which can reboot an Arduino mid-motion. Placing a **$0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ($100\text{ nF}$) ceramic decoupling capacitor** right next to the chip's `VCC` and `GND` pins (paired with a **$100\ \mu\text{F}$ electrolytic bulk capacitor** where power enters the breadboard) supplies that instant current burst locally and keeps the voltage rail rock-steady.

### Bridge Notes
When a capacitor $C$ (starting at $0\text{ V}$) charges toward $V_{\text{supply}}$ through a series resistor $R$, its voltage $V_C(t)$ at time $t$ follows an exponential curve governed by the **RC time constant** $\tau = R \cdot C$:

$$
\tau = R \cdot C, \qquad V_C(t) = V_{\text{supply}} \cdot \left(1 - e^{-t / \tau}\right)
$$

Check the units carefully: multiplying Ohms ($\Omega$) by Farads ($\text{F}$) produces **seconds ($\text{s}$)**:
- If $R = 10\text{ k}\Omega = 10{,}000\ \Omega$ and $C = 100\ \mu\text{F} = 0.000100\text{ F}$, then $\tau = 10{,}000 \times 0.000100 = 1.0\text{ s}$.
- At $t = 1\tau$ ($1.0\text{ s}$), the capacitor reaches $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of $V_{\text{supply}}$ ($3.16\text{ V}$ on a $5.0\text{ V}$ supply).
- At $t = 5\tau$ ($5.0\text{ s}$), the capacitor reaches $1 - e^{-5} \approx 99.3\%$ of $V_{\text{supply}}$ ($4.97\text{ V}$—effectively fully charged).

*(Safety note on polarized capacitors: Small ceramic decoupling capacitors like $0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ / $100\text{ nF}$ are **unpolarized** and can be inserted either way. Larger cylindrical aluminum **electrolytic** capacitors like $100\ \mu\text{F}$ are **polarized**: the shorter leg marked with a **`-` stripe** MUST connect to `GND`!)*

import math


def rc_time_constant_s(r_ohms, c_uf):
    c_f = c_uf / 1_000_000.0
    return r_ohms * c_f


def capacitor_charging_volts(v_supply, tau_s, t_s):
    return v_supply * (1.0 - math.exp(-t_s / tau_s))

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Wiring a polarized electrolytic capacitor backward (`-` stripe to `5V` instead of `GND`):** Reverse voltage breaks down the thin oxide layer inside an electrolytic capacitor, causing it to overheat and pop! Always connect the shorter lead with the **`-` stripe** to `GND`.
- **Placing the $0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ decoupling capacitor far away from the chip on the breadboard:** Long jumper wires add inductance that blocks fast current pulses; always place the $0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ceramic capacitor as close as physically possible to the IC's `VCC` and `GND` pins.

## Check yourself

1. **Unit conversion (`nF` to `µF`):** Ceramic decoupling capacitors are often labeled **`100 nF`** (100 nanofarads). Since $1\ \mu\text{F} = 1{,}000\text{ nF}$, how many microfarads (`µF`) is $100\text{ nF}$?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   $\frac{100\text{ nF}}{1000} = \mathbf{0.1\ \mu\text{F}}$. (`100 nF` and `0.1 µF` are two names for the exact same capacitor!)
   </details>

2. **Calculating $1\tau$ and $5\tau$:** A sensor filter uses a $1\text{ k}\Omega$ ($1{,}000\ \Omega$) resistor and a $10\ \mu\text{F}$ ($0.000010\text{ F}$) capacitor. What is the RC time constant $\tau = R \cdot C$ in milliseconds (`ms`), and how long does it take to reach $\approx 99.3\%$ ($5\tau$)?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   $\tau = 1{,}000\ \Omega \times 0.000010\text{ F} = 0.010\text{ s} = \mathbf{10\text{ ms}}$, so $5\tau = \mathbf{50\text{ ms}}$.
   </details>

3. **Why decoupling matters in robotics:** Your Arduino Uno runs fine when blinking an LED, but reboots every time a motor on the same power supply starts spinning. Which components across the power rails fix this brownout?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   A **$100\ \mu\text{F}$ bulk electrolytic capacitor** across the breadboard power rails (to buffer motor startup current) plus a **$0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ($100\text{ nF}$) ceramic decoupling capacitor** across the microcontroller's `VCC` and `GND` pins.
   </details>

## Recap
- A **capacitor** ($C$, in Farads `F`) stores charge and resists sudden changes in **voltage** ($V$), charging $63.2\%$ in one **RC time constant** $\tau = R \cdot C$ and $\approx 99.3\%$ in $5\tau$.
- **Decoupling capacitors** ($0.1\ \mu\text{F}$ ceramic near logic chips + $100\ \mu\text{F}$ electrolytic on power rails) act as local charge reservoirs that prevent supply voltage dips.
