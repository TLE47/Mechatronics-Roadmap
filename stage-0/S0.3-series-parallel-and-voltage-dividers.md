# Lesson S0.3: Series vs. Parallel Circuits, Voltage Dividers & Potentiometers

## Goal
Distinguish **series** connections (same current, split voltage) from **parallel** connections (same voltage, split current), and use the **voltage divider equation** ($V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$) to understand how 3-pin **potentiometers** and resistive sensors produce a variable voltage from $0\text{ V}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **Series vs. parallel resistor rules**: Why resistors connected end-to-end in a single chain add their resistances ($R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$) and share the same current, while resistors connected side-by-side across the same two nodes share the same voltage.
  2. **Voltage dividers ($V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$) and potentiometers**: Scaling a high voltage down to a safe microcontroller range using two series resistors, and how turning a 3-pin potentiometer knob slides its center wiper between `5V` and `GND`.
- **New syntax:** None.
- **Estimated time (untimed guesses):** Typical: 1.5–2.0 hours | Slow-learner: 2.5–3.5 hours

### Idea 2: Voltage Dividers ($V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$) and Potentiometers
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Voltage Dividers](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/voltage-dividers) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`Ideal Voltage Divider`**, **`The Circuit`**, **`The Equation`**, **`Simplifications`**, **`Applications`**, and **`Potentiometers`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip the resistive-divider loading proof at the very bottom until Track 01.
  - **Key takeaway:** When two resistors $R_1$ (top, connected to $V_{\text{in}}$) and $R_2$ (bottom, connected to `GND`) are wired in series, the midpoint node between them sits at $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$. A **potentiometer** is simply a single resistive track wired between `5V` and `GND` with a movable middle pin (the **wiper**) that splits the track into $R_1$ and $R_2$, letting you dial $V_{\text{out}}$ smoothly from $0.0\text{ V}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$.
  - **Alternative resource:** [Wokwi Docs — `wokwi-potentiometer` Reference](https://docs.wokwi.com/parts/wokwi-potentiometer) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]` — Read exact headings **`Pin names`** (`GND`, `SIG`, `VCC`) and **`Operation`**.

### Bridge Notes
Understanding **series** and **parallel** circuits comes down to two conservation laws (known as **Kirchhoff's Laws**):
1. **In a series loop**, charge has only one path, so current $I$ is identical everywhere ($I = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_1 + R_2}$), and the voltage drops around the loop sum to the supply voltage ($V_{\text{in}} = V_1 + V_2$).
2. **In a parallel circuit**, every branch connects directly across the same two nodes, so every branch sees the full voltage $V_{\text{in}}$ while the supply current splits among the branches ($I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2$).

Multiplying the series current $I = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_1 + R_2}$ by the bottom resistor $R_2$ gives the **voltage divider** formula:

$$
V_{\text{out}} = I \cdot R_2 = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

For example, if you want an Arduino Uno pin (which safely reads $0\text{ V}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$) to monitor a $15.0\text{ V}$ robot battery, choosing $R_1 = 20\text{ k}\Omega$ on top and $R_2 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ on the bottom scales $15.0\text{ V}$ down by $\frac{10}{20 + 10} = \frac{1}{3}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$! *(Simulator note: Because Wokwi does not simulate fixed-resistor analog dividers, test fixed-resistor dividers in **Falstad** (`Circuits -> Basics -> Voltage Divider`), and use Wokwi's **`wokwi-potentiometer`** when feeding a variable voltage to an Arduino Uno pin.)*

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Swapping $R_1$ (top) and $R_2$ (bottom) in the voltage divider formula:** Because $V_{\text{out}}$ is measured from the midpoint node to **`GND`** (across the **bottom** resistor $R_2$), the numerator is always **`R2_bottom`**, not `R1_top`! If you swap the $20\text{ k}\Omega$ and $10\text{ k}\Omega$ resistors on a $15\text{ V}$ battery ($R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ on top, $R_2 = 20\text{ k}\Omega$ on bottom), $V_{\text{out}}$ becomes $15 \times \frac{20}{30} = 10.0\text{ V}$—which will destroy a $5\text{ V}$ Arduino pin!
- **Using $10\ \Omega$ resistors instead of $10\text{ k}\Omega$ resistors in a voltage divider:** Two $10\ \Omega$ resistors ($20\ \Omega$ total) across $5\text{ V}$ give the same $2.5\text{ V}$ midpoint voltage as two $10\text{ k}\Omega$ resistors, **but** they waste $I = \frac{5\text{ V}}{20\ \Omega} = 250\text{ mA}$ ($1.25\text{ W}$ of heat!) instead of $0.25\text{ mA}$. For signal voltage dividers, choose resistors in the **$1\text{ k}\Omega$ to $10\text{ k}\Omega$** range.
- **Why you can't power a motor from a resistor voltage divider:** A voltage divider is only for **low-current sensor signals** (into an ADC pin that draws almost zero current). If you connect a heavy load like a motor across $R_2$, the motor's low resistance sits in parallel with $R_2$ and drags $V_{\text{out}}$ down toward $0\text{ V}$.

## Check yourself

1. **Equal-resistor voltage divider:** If you wire two identical $4.7\text{ k}\Omega$ resistors in series between `5V` and `GND`, what is the voltage at the midpoint between them?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   **`2.50 V`**. Whenever $R_1 = R_2$, the fraction $\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$, cutting the input voltage in half ($5.0\text{ V} \times 0.5 = 2.50\text{ V}$).
   </details>

2. **Swapped resistor danger:** You want to step a $12.0\text{ V}$ battery down to $4.0\text{ V}$ using a $20\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor and a $10\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor. Which resistor must connect to $+12.0\text{ V}$ ($R_1$, top), and which must connect to `GND` ($R_2$, bottom)? What voltage appears at $V_{\text{out}}$ if you accidentally swap them?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   - Top resistor $R_1 = \mathbf{20\text{ k}\Omega}$ (to $+12\text{ V}$) and bottom resistor $R_2 = \mathbf{10\text{ k}\Omega}$ (to `GND`), giving $12.0 \times \frac{10}{20 + 10} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ V}}$.
   - If you swap them ($R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ top, $R_2 = 20\text{ k}\Omega$ bottom), $V_{\text{out}} = 12.0 \times \frac{20}{10 + 20} = \mathbf{8.0\text{ V}}$, which exceeds the $5.0\text{ V}$ Arduino pin limit!
   </details>

3. **Potentiometer pins:** A rotary potentiometer has 3 pins: `GND` (left), `SIG` / wiper (middle), and `VCC` (right, connected to `5V`). What voltage appears on `SIG` when the knob is turned all the way to the `GND` end (`0%`), halfway (`50%`), and all the way to the `VCC` end (`100%`)?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   - At `0%` (`GND` end): **`0.0 V`**
   - At `50%` (halfway): **`2.5 V`**
   - At `100%` (`VCC` end): **`5.0 V`**
   </details>

## Recap
- **Series resistors** share the same current and add their resistances ($R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2$); **parallel branches** share the same voltage and add their currents ($I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2$).
- A **voltage divider** outputs $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$, and a **potentiometer** is an adjustable voltage divider whose center wiper pin sweeps smoothly from $0\text{ V}$ to $5.0\text{ V}$.
