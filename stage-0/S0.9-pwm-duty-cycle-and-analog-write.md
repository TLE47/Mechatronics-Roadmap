## Goal
Control effective power delivered to a load by switching a digital pin rapidly between $0\text{ V}$ and $5.0\text{ V}$ using **Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM)** ($V_{\text{avg}} = \text{Duty} \times 5.0\text{ V}$), inspect live PWM waveforms on **Falstad's oscilloscope**, and drive an LED across 4 brightness levels on Arduino Uno pin `~9` using 8-bit **`analogWrite(pin, 0..255)`** in Wokwi.

- **New ideas (max 2):**
  1. **Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM) duty cycle and average voltage ($V_{\text{avg}} = \text{Duty} \times V_{\text{cc}}$)**: Controlling average voltage and power using the fraction of each cycle period $T = 1/f$ that a fast digital switch stays `HIGH` ($T_{\text{on}} / T$).
  2. **8-bit Arduino PWM output (`analogWrite` `0..255`) and carrier frequency**: Mapping an 8-bit command ($2^8 = 256$ steps, `0` to `255`) to $0\%\text{–}100\%$ duty cycle on Arduino Uno tilde (`~`) pins (`3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11`).
- **New syntax (C/C++ on Arduino Uno):** `analogWrite(pwm_pin, duty_0_to_255)`.
- **Target time (unmeasured — [log yours](../.github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/beginner-timing-log.md)):** Typical: 1.5–2.0 hours | Slow-learner: 2.5–3.0 hours

## Learn

### Idea 1: Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM) duty cycle and average voltage ($V_{\text{avg}} = \text{Duty} \times V_{\text{cc}}$)
- **Primary resource:** [SparkFun — Pulse Width Modulation](https://learn.sparkfun.com/tutorials/pulse-width-modulation) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact headings **`What is Pulse-width Modulation?`**, **`Duty Cycle`**, and **`Examples`**.
  - **What to skip:** Skip manual ATmega timer-register bit-banging—use `analogWrite()` in Stage 0.
  - **Key takeaway:** An Arduino Uno has no true analog voltage output (`DAC`); its pins can only switch between $0.0\text{ V}$ (`LOW`) and $5.0\text{ V}$ (`HIGH`). To simulate an intermediate voltage, **Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM)** switches the pin between $0\text{ V}$ and $5\text{ V}$ hundreds of times per second. The **duty cycle** is the fraction (or percentage) of each period $T = 1/f$ that the signal stays `HIGH` ($\text{Duty} = T_{\text{on}} / T$). Because your eyes and mechanical motors average out fast pulses, a $25\%$ duty cycle acts like $0.25 \times 5.0\text{ V} = 1.25\text{ V}$, and a $50\%$ duty cycle acts like $2.50\text{ V}$!

### Idea 2: 8-bit Arduino PWM output (`analogWrite` `0..255`) and carrier frequency
- **Primary resource:** [Wokwi Docs — `wokwi-arduino-uno` Reference ("Pin names")](https://docs.wokwi.com/parts/wokwi-arduino-uno) `[HTTP 200 — Verified by scripts/check_links.py]`
  - **What to read:** Read exact heading **`Pin names`** and note the six digital pins (`3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11`) that support hardware `analogWrite` PWM.
  - **What to skip:** Skip `wokwi-logic-analyzer` for now (see note below).
  - **Key takeaway:** On the Arduino Uno, **`analogWrite(pin, value)`** works on the six hardware PWM pins (`3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11`) and accepts an **8-bit integer (`0` to `255`)**, where `0` is always off ($0\%$), `128` is $\approx 50.2\%$ duty cycle ($2.51\text{ V}$ average), and `255` is always on ($100\%$, $5.00\text{ V}$).
  - **Simulator waveform note (Falstad Scope vs. Wokwi Logic Analyzer):** Wokwi's `wokwi-logic-analyzer` does **not** display live waveforms in the browser—it records a `.vcd` file that only downloads when you stop the simulation and requires installing desktop software (`PulseView` or `GTKWave`) to view. To see live PWM square waves right in your browser with zero install, we use **Falstad's built-in Oscilloscope** (`falstad/S0.9-pwm-scope.txt`)!

### Bridge Notes
For any 8-bit command $\text{pwm-val} \in [0, 255]$ on a $5.0\text{ V}$ Arduino Uno, the **duty cycle**, **average voltage** $V_{\text{avg}}$, and **pulse ON-time** $T_{\text{on}}$ (at period $T = 1 / f_{\text{pwm}}$) are:

$$
\text{Duty} = \frac{\text{pwm-val}}{255.0}, \qquad V_{\text{avg}} = \text{Duty} \times 5.0\text{ V}, \qquad T_{\text{on}} = \text{Duty} \times \frac{1}{f_{\text{pwm}}}
$$

**Watch out — 10-bit `analogRead` (`0..1023`) vs. 8-bit `analogWrite` (`0..255`):**
- `analogRead(A0)` returns a **10-bit** number (`0` to `1023`).
- `analogWrite(9, val)` expects an **8-bit** number (`0` to `255`).
- Passing `1023` directly into `analogWrite(9, val)` wraps around modulo 256! Always divide a 10-bit ADC reading by `4` (`pwm_val = adc_raw / 4;`) to map `0..1023` cleanly onto `0..255`.

> **🔭 Looking Ahead (Stage 0 LED PWM vs. Track 01/03 Motor PWM Frequency):**
> On the Arduino Uno, default `analogWrite()` runs at **`~490 Hz`** on pins `3, 9, 10, 11` ($T \approx 2{,}040.8\ \mu\text{s} \approx 2.04\text{ ms}$) and **`~980 Hz`** on pins `5, 6` ($T \approx 1{,}020.4\ \mu\text{s}$). That is plenty fast enough for dimming LEDs without visible flicker (human eyes fuse flashes above $\sim 60\text{ Hz}$). However, when we drive real motors in **Track 01 and Track 03**, a $490\text{ Hz}$ or $980\text{ Hz}$ switching wave vibrates the motor windings right in the middle of human hearing ($20\text{ Hz–}20\text{ kHz}$), making an annoying audible whine and causing high current ripple. In Track 03, you will configure hardware timers for **`20 kHz` ultrasonic PWM** ($T = 50\ \mu\text{s}$) so motor switching is completely silent!

## Common mistakes & troubleshooting
- **Calling `analogWrite()` on a non-PWM pin like `Pin 2`, `Pin 4`, `Pin 7`, `Pin 8`, `Pin 12`, or `Pin 13`:** On the Arduino Uno, hardware PWM only exists on the six tilde (`~`) pins: **`3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11`**. On any other digital pin, `analogWrite(pin, val)` simply outputs `LOW` for values `< 128` and `HIGH` for values `>= 128`!
- **Passing `analogRead(A0)` (`0..1023`) directly into `analogWrite(9, val)` (`0..255`):** Values above `255` wrap around modulo `256` (`256` becomes `0`!). Always scale `0..1023` down to `0..255` by dividing by `4` (`adc_raw / 4`).

## Check yourself

1. **Instantaneous voltage vs. average voltage:** When `analogWrite(9, 128)` ($\approx 50\%$ duty cycle) runs on a $5.0\text{ V}$ Arduino Uno, does Pin 9 ever sit at a steady $2.50\text{ V}$, or does it switch rapidly between $0.0\text{ V}$ and $5.0\text{ V}$?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   It **switches rapidly between $0.0\text{ V}$ and $5.0\text{ V}$** (spending $\approx 50\%$ of each $2{,}040.8\ \mu\text{s}$ cycle at $5.0\text{ V}$ and $50\%$ at $0.0\text{ V}$), which averages out to $2.51\text{ V}$.
   </details>

2. **Scaling 10-bit ADC to 8-bit PWM:** If `analogRead(A0)` returns `768` ($75\%$ of `1023`), what is `768 / 4`, and what average voltage does `analogWrite(9, 768 / 4)` produce?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   `768 / 4 = 192`, which produces $V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{192}{255} \times 5.0\text{ V} \approx \mathbf{3.76\text{ V}}$.
   </details>

3. **490 Hz LED PWM vs. 20 kHz Motor PWM:** Why is the Arduino Uno's default $490\text{ Hz}$ `analogWrite()` frequency fine for dimming an LED in Stage 0, whereas motor controllers in Track 03 use $20\text{ kHz}$ PWM?
   <details>
   <summary><strong>Answer</strong></summary>

   Human eyes cannot see flicker above $\sim 60\text{ Hz}$, so $490\text{ Hz}$ looks completely smooth on an LED. However, human ears hear vibrations up to $20\text{ kHz}$, so $490\text{ Hz}$ makes motor coils whine audibly and causes large current ripple, whereas $20\text{ kHz}$ is ultrasonic (silent) and keeps motor current smooth.
   </details>

- On the Arduino Uno, **`analogWrite(pin, 0..255)`** outputs 8-bit PWM on pins **`3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11`**.
